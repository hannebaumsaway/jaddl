# JADDL article voice

Drafted from 196 published articles. This describes what the existing recaps
already do — it is documentation, not a new direction. Correct anything that
reads wrong; the file is the source of truth once it does.

Pair it with a game brief (`pnpm brief --year Y --week W --team "Name"`). The
brief supplies every league fact. This file supplies only how to write.

## Length

400–650 words. The median published article is 499. Do not pad to reach a
number; a decisive blowout can be shorter than a close game because there is
less to explain.

## Shape

Nearly every recap follows the same six beats. Keep them, but do not make the
seams visible.

1. **Result in one sentence, with a twist clause.** State winner, loser and both
   exact scores, then turn on "which is" and land the joke.
   > The Red Hornets beat the Lanniesters 169.8-127.0 in Week 7, which is the
   > kind of game where one team rode a historic fourth-quarter comeback and the
   > other team started a defense that scored negative points.

2. **The winner's case.** Walk the top scorers with exact point totals, each
   carrying a small aside. Facts first, comment second — never comment alone.

3. **"Meanwhile," the loser.** Concede their good performances honestly — the
   recurring register is "perfectly respectable," "perfectly fine," "acceptable"
   — then pivot to where the week actually died.

4. **Name the villain.** One player or lineup slot takes the weight. The -1.0
   Washington defense. Montgomery's 1.2. This is usually the most quotable
   paragraph; give it room.

5. **Consequence.** Updated record, the all-time series, what it means for the
   playoff picture. Short.

6. **The closer.** Almost always: *"Welcome to Week N, where [owner] learned
   that [the lesson]."* It is a signature. Use it unless a better ending
   presents itself.

## Voice

- **Owners by first name, teams by team name**, used interchangeably: "Jacob's
  Monkeys," "Dan's Hornets," "Lannie's Lanniesters." The brief supplies both.
- **Exact decimals, never rounded.** 42.75, not 42.8. 194.50, not 194.5. The
  precision is part of the joke.
- **Sentence fragments for the punchline.** "Should be. Wasn't." "This was not
  most weeks." "The Commanders. In Week 4."
- **Restate a big number to make it land.** "Thirty-three points. In one
  quarter. After scoring zero in the previous three."
- **Analogies drawn wide of football.** Paying someone to punch you in the face.
  A butter knife to a gunfight. A red wedding. Reach outside the sport.
- **Profanity, sparingly.** Roughly once an article at most, as a rhythm break —
  "went fucking ballistic," "ass-kicking." Never in the opening sentence.
- **Second person for the universal move.** "when your three running backs
  combine for 23.4 points."
- **Deadpan over exclamation.** The funniest lines are delivered flat. No
  exclamation marks.
- **Subtitles can break the fourth wall.** "Have I used that pun before?"

## Invented quotes

The published articles put words in owners' mouths — a fabricated quote,
attributed by first name, usually one per article, placed after the beat it
comments on.

> "I thought we'd win by maybe 10 or 15 points," Jacob said afterward, which is
> the kind of thing you say when you're trying to sound humble but secretly knew
> this was going to be a massacre.

This is an established convention of the column and everyone in the league is
in on it. Keep the invented quote plausible and never hostile. Quotes from real
NFL players must be **real** or omitted — do not fabricate those.

## Facts

- Everything about the league comes from the brief. Records, series history,
  clinches, franchise counts, lineups, multipliers.
- Do not invent league facts. If a stat would strengthen a line and it is not in
  the brief, leave the line out or write `[CHECK: …]` for a human to resolve.
- The brief's `angles` are candidate storylines, not sentences. Pick the one or
  two that carry the piece and ignore the rest. Never list them.
- A multiplier of 2.0x or more is a genuine outlier, about 1.0x is a normal
  week, below 0.5x is why someone lost, and `—` means there is not enough
  history to judge — say nothing about that player's form.

## Real-world NFL context

The published articles lean hard on what actually happened in the NFL that week
— Bo Nix being shut out for three quarters then throwing for 33 fourth-quarter
points, DeVonta Smith calling his own shot at halftime. That texture is a large
part of why they read well.

**The brief does not contain any of it.** It knows Bo Nix scored 42.75; it does
not know how. Anyone writing from the brief alone will produce a competent recap
with no outside colour.

So: use real NFL detail where it is genuinely known, and leave it out where it
is not. An invented NFL narrative is the one failure mode that would embarrass
the column, because it is checkable by everyone reading.
